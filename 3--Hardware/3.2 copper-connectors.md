## Modular (Telephone/Ethernet) Connectors

### RJ11

- **Full name:** Registered Jack type 11
- **Positions/Conductors:** 6-position, 2-conductor (6P2C) — sometimes 4 conductors (technically RJ14, but still called RJ11)
- **Use:** Analog telephone lines, DSL (Digital Subscriber Line) connections
- **Size:** Smaller than RJ45

> **Common mistake:** RJ11 can accidentally be inserted into an RJ45 port because the RJ11 is small enough to fit. An RJ45 connector is too large to fit into an RJ11 port. Always verify the correct port when working behind a device.

### RJ45

- **Full name:** Registered Jack type 45
- **Positions/Conductors:** 8-position, 8-conductor (8P8C)
- **Use:** Ethernet networks (most common), serial connections, and other applications
- **Size:** Larger than RJ11; also called a "modular connector"

Both connectors look similar at a glance — RJ11 is slightly narrower. Always confirm the correct connection type before inserting.

## Coaxial Connectors

### F Connector

- **Type:** Coaxial (threaded)
- **Use:** Cable television, cable modem (DOCSIS) connections
- **Design:** Threaded collar screws onto the port to prevent accidental disconnection
- **Center pin:** A single copper conductor in the middle of the coax carries the signal

The F connector is what connects your coaxial cable from the wall or cable provider to the back of a cable modem or TV.

## Punch-Down Blocks

Punch-down blocks terminate large numbers of twisted pair wires quickly and without individual connectors on each wire.

**How they work:**
- Individual wires are placed into slots on the block
- A punch-down tool presses the wire down, and small metal contacts **pierce the insulation** to make contact with the copper inside
- The tool simultaneously trims the excess wire

**Advantages:**
- Fast — no crimping individual connectors
- Inexpensive at scale
- Used extensively on the back of patch panels in wiring closets

## USB Connectors

USB has evolved through several connector types across different versions:

| Connector | USB Version | Description | Common Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard-A** | 1.1, 2.0, 3.0 | Flat rectangular plug | Host side (computer, charger) |
| **Standard-B** | 1.1/2.0 | Square plug | Printers, older peripherals |
| **Standard-B** | 3.0 | Different shape from 2.0 B | Newer peripherals |
| **Mini-B** | 2.0 | Small; older mobile devices | Older phones, cameras |
| **Micro-B** | 2.0 | Very small flat plug | Older Android phones, tablets |
| **Micro-B** | 3.0 | Different/wider than 2.0 Micro-B | External hard drives |
| **USB-C** | 3.1+ | Symmetrical; reversible; 24-pin | Modern standard; all device types |

**USB-C key point:** USB-C is a **physical connector standard**, not a speed standard. The same USB-C port can carry USB data, Thunderbolt, HDMI, DisplayPort, and power — depending on what the device supports.

> **Exam Tip:** Know that Micro-B and Standard-B look different between USB 2.0 and USB 3.0 versions. USB-C consolidates all of these into one universal connector.

## Power Connectors

### Molex

- **Pins:** 4 pins
- **Voltages:** 12V and 5V DC
- **Use:** Powers older internal storage drives (HDDs), fans, and other peripherals inside desktop computers
- **Also called:** AMP Mate-n-Lok
- **Connection:** Friction-fit; no locking clip — pulls apart without tools

Molex connectors are common on older power supplies alongside the main motherboard power connector. Newer drives use SATA power instead, but Molex is still found in older systems.

## Proprietary & Legacy Connectors

### Lightning (Apple)

- **Pins:** 8
- **Use:** Older iPhones, iPads, iPods — Apple proprietary
- **Reversible:** Yes — inserts either way
- **Why it was introduced:** More power delivery than Micro-USB; reversible design; compact

Lightning was Apple's answer to Micro-USB's limitations. The industry has since moved toward USB-C, and newer Apple devices now use USB-C. Older Apple devices still use Lightning.

### DB-9 / RS-232

- **Full name:** DB-9 = D-subminiature 9-pin connector; RS-232 = Recommended Standard 232 (serial communication standard from ~1969)
- **Pins:** 9
- **Use:** Serial console access to network infrastructure — switches, routers, firewalls
- **Modern relevance:** Largely replaced by USB for general use, but still present as a **console port** on many enterprise network devices

If you need to directly configure a switch, router, or firewall at the command line, the **console port** is often a DB-9 (RS-232) connection. Many IT bags still carry a DB-9 cable or a USB-to-DB-9 adapter for this purpose.

## Connector Quick Reference

| Connector | Pins | Signal Type | Primary Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RJ11** | 6P2C (or 4C) | Analog / DSL | Telephone, DSL modem |
| **RJ45** | 8P8C | Ethernet / serial | Ethernet networking |
| **F connector** | 1 (center pin) | Coaxial RF | Cable TV, cable modem |
| **USB-A** | 4 (2.0) / 9 (3.0) | USB data/power | Host-side USB |
| **USB-C** | 24 | USB / TB / HDMI / DP | Modern universal connector |
| **Molex** | 4 | 12V / 5V DC power | Internal peripheral power |
| **Lightning** | 8 | Proprietary | Older Apple devices |
| **DB-9** | 9 | RS-232 serial | Console port on network gear |
